# Theory baselines should anchor four ML questions

The repo should add four ML questions, in this order: **Q9**, next-trade-sign prediction scored against the linear predictor of a long-memory sign series; **Q10**, a cross-year transfer matrix with drift attribution built on Q9's trade-only features; **Q11**, a 2023-only mid-price study (train June 2023, test July 2023) that tests the "only the surprise moves the price" relation; and **Q12**, a flexible-kernel Hawkes extension with a small neural point process used only as a predictability ceiling. The models are deliberately plain: L2 logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees for Q9 to Q11, and a GRU with a log-normal-mixture head for Q12. The independent benchmark literature finds that deep order-book models lose most of their headline skill on fresh data, that input representation matters more than architecture, and that neural point processes stop beating Hawkes processes once the Hawkes kernel is made flexible. The evaluation protocol matters more than the model: purged walk-forward on whole days, unsmoothed labels, fold-local preprocessing, a strictly-before trade and quote join, day-block bootstrap intervals, a trial log, and one test month scored once. The honest public claim available is narrow but defensible: "we measured how much an ML model adds over a theory-derived baseline, how that increment changed from 2023 to 2026, and why." The claims that are not available are any statement of profitability, any accuracy number compared with 50% instead of with the baseline ladder, and any mid-price result outside 2023. One caveat applies to the whole report: the research notes were compiled in a session where only web search worked and full-text fetches were blocked, so every literature claim below carries a verification tag and many specific numbers remain unchecked against the primary papers.

## How to read the verification tags

The notes used three slightly different tag sets; this report merges them into one. **[V]** means the claim was confirmed this session from a search-result snippet or abstract of the cited source. It does not mean the full paper was read, because direct fetches of arxiv.org, ar5iv, Springer, binance.com and similar hosts were blocked. **[2nd]** means the claim came from a secondary summary (an aggregator, a third-party review, a blog) and was not checked against the primary source. **[R]** means the claim was recalled from the researcher's prior reading of the cited paper and was not re-verified this session; any number tagged [R] should be checked against the paper before it is quoted publicly. **[I]** marks an inference, derivation or design recommendation made in the notes or in this synthesis; it is not a literature result. Nothing in the notes was measured on the repo's own data, and all throughput and memory figures are unmeasured estimates.

Two project facts shape everything that follows. Trade data (aggTrades) exists for 2023 to 2026, but top-of-book quotes (bookTicker) exist only for 2023. Therefore every cross-year analysis must use features computed from trades alone, and every analysis that needs a mid-price is confined to 2023. Compute is 4 CPU cores and 15 GB of RAM, and the repo's declared dependencies are currently only polars, numpy, httpx and matplotlib, so scikit-learn, LightGBM and PyTorch would all be new dependencies (checked in `pyproject.toml` this session).

## Published accuracy collapses on fresh data, and theory already predicts sign skill

### Deep order-book models: the headline numbers do not survive

The famous results come from one small dataset. DeepLOB reported F1 scores of 83.40, 72.82 and 80.35 at horizons of 10, 20 and 50 events on FI-2010 [V, quoted in a later paper's comparison table] ([Tran et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.00983); primary: [Zhang et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)). FI-2010 is ten days of down-sampled data for five Finnish stocks from 2010, a limit the DeepLOB authors themselves state [V] ([DeepLOB](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.03668)). The LOBCAST benchmark re-implemented 15 deep models and concluded that "all models exhibit a significant performance drop when exposed to new data" [V] ([Prata et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). The best FI-2010 model, BiN-CTABL, lost about **19.6% F1** on average on newer NASDAQ data [V] ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.01915)). One note also reports all models landing at 48 to 61% F1 on the new data, tagged [V] there, while another note marks the same range [2nd] and unreconciled with the 19.6% figure; quote the qualitative finding, and check Table 6 of the paper before quoting the range. These are three-class scores, so the uninformed reference is about 33%, not 50%.

A second benchmark explains where the remaining skill lives. Briola, Bartolucci and Aste ran DeepLOB on 15 NASDAQ stocks over three years and found that **large-tick stocks are more predictable than small-tick stocks** [V] ([Briola et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09267)). Average MCC for small-tick stocks is 0.11 at a 10-update horizon and near zero at 100 updates [V] ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12315853/)); the matching large-tick figure of about 0.29 comes only from an aggregator [2nd] ([summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2403.09267)). Their conclusion is that "high forecasting power does not necessarily correspond to actionable trading signals" [V] ([Briola et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.09267v2)), and they report a case with MCC of 0.59 where not one complete open-and-close transaction was captured [V] ([Briola et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.09267v4)). The newer TLOB transformer reports the same two negatives from the inside: predictability declined over time by 6.68 F1 points, and performance deteriorated when the label threshold was set to the average spread [V] ([Berti and Kasneci](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15757)).

### Inputs beat architectures

The best-supported positive finding concerns features. Kolm, Turiel and Westray, on 115 Nasdaq stocks, found that "models trained on order flow significantly outperform most models trained directly on order books," that forecast accuracy peaks at about two average price changes and then declines, and that stocks with more book updates per price change are easier to predict [V] ([Kolm et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3900141)). An independent, non-peer-reviewed replication on IEX data reports out-of-sample R-squared of 0.35% for a CNN-LSTM against 0.27% for a linear ridge model [V] ([GitHub replication](https://github.com/Sudarikovamaria/DEEP-ORDER-FLOW-IMBALANCE-EXTRACTING-ALPHA-AT-MULTIPLE-HORIZONS-FROM-THE-LIMIT-ORDER-BOOK)), so the deep model's edge over the linear one was under a tenth of a percentage point. Gould and Bonart showed that a logistic regression on top-of-book queue imbalance predicts the direction of the next mid-price move, with "considerable" improvement for large-tick stocks and "moderate" for small-tick, and that more flexible fits only "slightly outperform" the logistic [V] ([Gould and Bonart](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/1512.03492.html)). On Binance perpetuals specifically, a 2026 preprint using boosted trees on 1-second data for five coins ranks order flow imbalance, spread and VWAP-to-mid deviation as the top features, with similar response shapes across assets [V, abstract level; headline metrics not retrieved] ([Bieganowski and Slepaczuk](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00776)).

One distinction must be kept straight when writing publicly. Order flow imbalance explains the price change over the *same* window with a large R-squared; this is an impact relation, not a forecast. The commonly quoted 65% figure for Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov is [R] and could not be confirmed this session ([Cont et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)); the repo's own Q3 already measures this contemporaneous relation. Predictive R-squared for returns is well under 1% in the one replication with numbers. The notes found no peer-reviewed head-to-head of boosted trees against DeepLOB on identical top-of-book inputs, and no peer-reviewed out-of-sample sign or mid-direction accuracy for Binance USDT-M perpetuals from aggTrades and bookTicker alone.

### The linear long-memory predictor is the baseline to beat

Order signs are a long-memory process. Lillo and Farmer found a sign autocorrelation decaying as a power law with exponent about 0.6 on the London Stock Exchange, and noted this would make signs "fairly predictable" and imply inefficiency, were it not offset by liquidity adjusting so that returns stay nearly uncorrelated [V] ([Lillo and Farmer](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0311053)). The accepted origin is the splitting of large hidden orders [V] ([Lillo, Mike, Farmer](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0412708)). Bouchaud, Farmer and Lillo show that if liquidity providers use a linear predictor of the next sign, the "surprise" impact model, in which only the unpredicted part of the sign moves the price, is mathematically equivalent to the propagator model the repo already estimates [V] ([Bouchaud, Farmer, Lillo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0809.0822)). The exponent relation beta = (1 - gamma)/2 for critical balance is [R] in the notes, though the repo's Q5 already tests it empirically. Taranto and coauthors found that the surprise-based model fits only slightly better than the raw-sign propagator, and that both need a split into price-changing and non-price-changing events, especially for large-tick assets [V] ([Taranto et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.02735)).

This gives a baseline derived from theory, and its construction is simple enough to explain line by line [I]. Take the measured sign autocorrelation C(lag). The best linear predictor of the next sign from the past p signs has weights that solve the Yule-Walker equations, which the Levinson-Durbin recursion solves quickly. Because a sign is plus or minus one, its conditional expectation equals 2P(buy) - 1, so the linear prediction converts directly to a probability. Under the DAR(p) sign model, where each sign is either copied from a past sign or drawn fresh, this linear form is the exact conditional expectation and not an approximation [V] ([Taranto et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1403.0842)). The notes derive that for a pure FARIMA process with gamma between 0.3 and 0.6 this predictor implies roughly 62 to 74% accuracy [I], but two caveats attach: real sign series have a free prefactor that FARIMA ties to gamma, so the filter must be computed from the empirical autocorrelation; and in hard-classification accuracy the trivial rule "repeat the last sign," with accuracy exactly (1 + C(1))/2, captures nearly all of it. The long filter earns its keep in probabilistic scores such as log-loss and calibration. The repo's Q1 already reports gamma near 0.38 for BTC and 0.24 for ETH, so high sign accuracy is the expected outcome here, not a discovery.

The notes found **no published study benchmarking gradient boosting or deep nets against this filter on anonymous market-wide trade signs** [I; absence after a limited search, not proof of absence]. The nearest item is a kinetic Ising model for identified traders that beat an autoregressive logistic benchmark by 5 to over 10 points with about 60% average accuracy [V] ([arXiv 1909.10807](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.10807)). An LSTM on Binance BTC-USDT trades claims over 60%, with a class-skew caveat [V] ([arXiv 2010.07404](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.07404)). That gap is the opening for Q9.

| Rung | Baseline or model | Information used | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Majority class | None | Guards against class-imbalance artifacts |
| 1 | Repeat last sign | One lag | Accuracy is exactly (1 + C(1))/2 and equals 1 minus the flip probability |
| 2 | Yule-Walker AR(p), p in the hundreds or more | Sign history | The theory-derived optimal linear filter |
| 3 | L2 logistic regression on lagged signs | Sign history | Same information as rung 2, fitted by likelihood; a fair comparator |
| 4 | L2 logistic and boosted trees on trade-only features | Signs, sizes, durations, flow imbalance | The ML increment that can be tracked across 2023 to 2026 |
| 5 | Rung 4 plus top-of-book state (2023 only) | Adds queue imbalance, spread, OFI | How much the book knows beyond the tape |

The scientific quantity in Q9 is the increment from rung 2 to rung 4, and in Q11 from rung 4 to rung 5. All rungs and expected ranges in this table are design choices and priors [I], not literature values.

### Why sign skill is not price skill

Sign persistence is public, so it is already priced. That is the content of the surprise model, and it means a model's sign accuracy measures how well it recovers known flow structure, not alpha [V for the mechanism] ([Bouchaud, Farmer, Lillo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0809.0822)). For mid-price direction on BTC and ETH perpetuals, which the notes judge to be large-tick instruments with the spread pinned at one tick [I; must be measured from the 2023 bookTicker data], queue imbalance should predict the next mid move well, but the move is one tick. Regular-tier Binance USDT-M fees are reported as 0.02% maker and 0.05% taker by third-party pages that agree with each other [V for the third-party pages; the official table was not opened] ([Finder](https://www.finder.com/cryptocurrency/trading/binance-futures-fees); [official schedule, not opened](https://www.binance.com/en/fee/futureFee)). A taker round trip therefore costs about 10 basis points in fees, orders of magnitude above a one-tick move, so on this venue the fee and not the spread is the binding cost [I].

## Leakage control is a protocol, not a library call

### Splits

The primary scheme should be walk-forward on whole UTC days with a gap, because it is the only scheme in which every prediction uses strictly past data. Scikit-learn's `TimeSeriesSplit` has a `gap` argument that defaults to zero, which means no purging unless it is set [R] ([scikit-learn docs](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.TimeSeriesSplit.html)). Purging removes any training sample whose label window overlaps a test label window, and an embargo additionally drops training samples just after a test block; both come from Lopez de Prado's book, with the specific formulas tagged [R] ([Lopez de Prado 2018](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Advances+in+Financial+Machine+Learning-p-9781119482086)) and the general description confirmed [V] ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purged_cross-validation)). His 1% embargo rule is a convention, and at tick scale it is meaningless [I]. The notes recommend instead recording for each sample the decision time and the time at which its label closes, purging by timestamp overlap and never by a fixed row count, and setting the gap to at least the longest horizon (100 events) plus the longest feature look-back [I].

Combinatorial purged cross-validation should be at most a secondary tool on the development month. Its supporting evidence, a study reporting lower overfitting probability than walk-forward, was run on synthetic data [V] ([Arian et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.knosys.2024.112477)). It trains on data that follow the test block, so the model has seen the future volatility and regime level; its paths share most of their data and are not independent [I]. A number from it must never be presented as a backtest.

The project's data shape dictates the calendar [I]. For Q11, June 2023 is the development period: all tuning happens by walk-forward inside June, and July 2023 is the lock-box, scored once by a script committed before it runs. For Q9 and Q10, each month is split chronologically inside itself (for example about 21 days of training, at least one day of embargo, about 7 days of test) so that same-month and cross-year numbers are equally out of sample.

### Labels

Smoothed labels are the main reason published scores are high. The FI-2010 label compares the current mid with the mean of the next k mids [V] ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)), and DeepLOB's LSE label compares the mean of the next k mids with the mean of the previous k [R] ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)). The LOBCAST journal version calls the labelling "prone to instability" [V] ([Prata et al. 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-024-10715-4)). The argument that half of the doubly-smoothed label is computable from the model's own input window is the notes' inference from the formulas, not a statement found in LOBCAST [I]. The repo should use a raw point-to-point label: the mid prevailing just before trade i+h minus the mid just before the decision, in ticks. It should never use the trade price as a target, because bid-ask bounce makes trade-price changes negatively autocorrelated with no economic content [R] ([Roll 1984](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1984.tb03897.x)).

Three further label rules follow [I]. A single aggressive order that sweeps several price levels prints several aggTrades with the same timestamp and side, so horizons counted on raw prints make h = 1 trivially predictable; the repo's existing Q0 aggregation to one event per aggressor order should be the event definition, with the raw version reported as a robustness check. Because most short-horizon mid labels on a one-tick-spread contract are "no change," report the unconditional three-class result and separately a "direction given a move" result, labelled as conditioned on the future. Finally, any class threshold must be fixed from training data or set in ticks beforehand, and class frequencies reported per fold.

### Features, joins and latency

Every scaler, threshold and selection step must be fitted inside the training window of each fold; fitting on train and test together is a named category in the standard leakage taxonomy [R] ([Kapoor and Narayanan 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804)), and Briola and coauthors describe a standardization that "prevents any data leakage" [V] ([Briola et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.09267v3)). The notes recommend features that are stationary by construction (ratios, values in ticks, sizes relative to a trailing median), rolling statistics shifted by one event, and one unit test that catches most look-ahead bugs: features at row i must be unchanged when every later row is replaced with garbage [I].

For the 2023 quote join, the classic lesson from Lee and Ready is that quotes can be recorded ahead of the trades that caused them, so a trade must be matched to an older quote [V] ([Lee and Ready 1991](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:46:y:1991:i:2:p:733-46)). On Binance, transaction time T is the matching-engine time and event time E is when the message was generated, according to developer-forum wording and not the official specification [V] ([Binance developer forum](https://dev.binance.vision/t/meaning-of-event-time-transaction-time-fields/5449)). The recommended rule is to join each trade to the last bookTicker row with T strictly earlier than the trade's T, and to verify the join daily by checking that taker buys print at or above the prior ask and taker sells at or below the prior bid [I]. Whether a same-millisecond quote is before or after the trade is an empirical question for this dataset that no source answered. The aggTrade and bookTicker field layouts in the notes are [R]. Public dumps carry no receive timestamps, so results should be reported as a function of an assumed delay; a signal that exists only at zero delay is a description of the book and not a strategy [I].

### Metrics, uncertainty and multiple testing

Accuracy alone misleads under class imbalance. MCC is high only when all four confusion-matrix cells are good [R] ([Chicco and Jurman 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-019-6413-7)), and log-loss and the Brier score are proper scoring rules that reward calibration [R] ([Gneiting and Raftery 2007](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)). The headline metric should be log-loss skill relative to each rung of the ladder, with MCC, AUC and a reliability curve alongside [I]. For Q11, add the mean signed forward mid return by prediction decile and the share of h-event moves that exceed spread plus two taker fees, which is the base rate of there being anything to trade [I].

Per-event standard errors are invalid here because overlapping h-event labels are serially dependent by construction. The default should be a paired bootstrap over whole days (or symbol-days), recomputing each metric per replicate; the stationary bootstrap with automatic block length is a within-day check [V] ([Politis and White 2004](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:emetrv:v:23:y:2004:i:1:p:53-70); [Patton, Politis, White 2009](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~ap172/Patton_Politis_White_2009.pdf)). For pairwise forecast comparison, the Diebold-Mariano test on daily mean loss differences is the clean option; its formulas and the small-sample correction are [R] ([Diebold and Mariano 1995](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.1995.10524599)). With about 30 days per month, the notes warn that day-level intervals are weak and that this should be said [I].

Every configuration tried should be written to an append-only trial log, and the count reported in each results table [I]. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio and the Probability of Backtest Overfitting exist for Sharpe-type statistics [V via secondary pages; verify formulas against the papers] ([Bailey and Lopez de Prado](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551); [Bailey et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)), but this project should not report a Sharpe ratio at all. For classification skill, a Holm-adjusted interval across the pre-declared family of horizons and symbols is the proportionate correction [I].

## Five snapshot months can separate a stale model from a less predictable market

### What to measure

The taxonomy is standard. Covariate shift is a change in the feature distribution, label shift a change in class frequencies, and concept shift a change in the feature-to-label relationship; definitions are [R] ([Moreno-Torres et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patcog.2011.06.019); [Gama et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2523813)). Because labels exist in every period, concept shift is directly testable here. Next-trade sign has class balance near one half, so label shift is negligible; what moved is the sign-persistence rate, and since lagged sign is a feature, that movement is concept shift [I]. This is exactly the repo's Q8 finding, restated in ML vocabulary.

The core artifact is a **transfer matrix** M[i, j]: the score of a model trained on month i and tested on held-out data of month j, over the regime months the repo already uses (June 2023, July 2023, and July of 2024, 2025 and 2026). The reporting convention of showing in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance side by side comes from the WILDS benchmark [R] ([Koh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07421)). The temporal version, Wild-Time, found "an average performance drop of 20% from in-distribution to out-of-distribution data" that 13 methods could not close [V] ([Yao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14238)). Two derived quantities do the explanatory work [I]. The **staleness gap** G_j = M[j, j] - M[2023, j] says how much a freshly trained model beats the old one on the same data. The **predictability change** D_j = M[j, j] - M[2023, 2023] says whether the task itself got harder for everyone. A raw drop in the 2023 model's score mixes the two; reporting them separately distinguishes "the model went stale" from "the signal was competed away," which is the alpha-decay story that McLean and Pontiff tell for 97 equity return predictors [V for the study design; their headline percentages are R and unconfirmed] ([McLean and Pontiff 2016](https://www.gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf)).

All skill must be stated relative to a baseline evaluated in the same period [I]. "Repeat the last sign" has accuracy equal to one minus the flip probability, which the repo already knows moved; raw accuracy changes would mostly restate Q8. June to July 2023 serves as the reference for ordinary one-month drift, and later gaps are reported as multiples of it.

### How to attribute

The recommended attribution has two layers [I]. First, train a classifier to tell month A from month B using scale-free features; its AUC is a single effect size for covariate shift (the classifier two-sample test, [R], [Lopez-Paz and Oquab](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.06545)), and its output probability supplies weights. The DISDE method then splits the performance drop into three terms: harder but familiar examples, a changed feature-to-label relationship on shared support, and examples the training month never saw; it estimates them "by reweighting data from P and Q to approximate samples from the shared distribution" [V for the structure and quotes] ([Cai, Namkoong, Yadlowsky](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02011)). The specific weight formulas in the notes were derived, not quoted, and must be checked against the paper before implementation [I]. Second, refit the same small logistic model in each month and test coefficient equality with symbol-day clustered errors; this says which relationships moved, in a form an undergraduate can defend term by term [I]. Related tabular evidence argues that changes in the feature-to-label relationship, not in the features, drive most real-world degradation [R; title confirmed V] ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.05284v6)).

Two cheap extras sharpen the story [I]. Permutation importance of the frozen 2023 model, measured in loss on a later month, flags features whose relationship to the label broke; SHAP values of a frozen model compared across months show only that inputs moved and cannot reveal concept shift. And appending regime descriptors (trades per second, relative tick size) to the feature set tests whether the "concept shift" shrinks, which would mean the same physics at a different operating point. Tick-size changes offer a quasi-experiment: Binance announced batches of USD-M tick-size updates on many dates between August 2023 and March 2026 [V] ([2023-08-29 announcement](https://www.binance.com/en/support/announcement/updates-on-tick-size-for-multiple-usd%E2%93%A2-m-perpetual-futures-contracts-2023-08-29-8764f0d84e6b4c7abc97f036bb6f9ebc); [2025-12-22](https://www.binance.com/en/support/announcement/1cd08ee9bafd40a2a94e8dfc58408f82)), so degradation can be compared between symbols whose tick changed and those whose did not.

Streaming detectors such as ADWIN, DDM and Page-Hinkley are the wrong primary tool. They are built to find an unknown change time in a stream [V via library documentation] ([CapyMOA](https://capymoa.org/notebooks/drift_detection.html)), whereas here the change points are fixed by the sampling design; with millions of dependent events they would fire constantly [I]. With weights that explode when months barely overlap, with trade-only features, and with five snapshots, the design also has hard limits: it cannot tell gradual from abrupt change, cannot estimate a retraining cadence finer than a year, and inherits any timestamp-resolution difference across years as spurious drift in duration features unless times are coarsened to a common resolution [I].

The literature leaves this question open. Sirignano and Cont report "remarkably stable out-of-sample prediction accuracy across time" for a pooled deep model on US equities [V, abstract] ([Sirignano and Cont](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.06917v1)); LOBCAST reports large drops; the Binance preprint shows stability across assets, not across time. The notes found no study measuring year-over-year decay of sign or direction models on Binance perpetuals.

## A flexible Hawkes kernel comes before any neural point process

The repo's branching-ratio ladder (0.71, 0.85, 0.96) reproduces a known dispute. Hardiman, Bercot and Bouchaud fitted power-law kernels to E-mini futures and found the kernel integrates to about one, attributing lower estimates to exponential kernels on short windows [V] ([Hardiman et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.1405)). Filimonov and Sornette replied that outliers, edge effects and non-stationarity bias the estimate upward, and that regime-switching Poisson processes produce spurious near-critical values [V] ([Filimonov and Sornette 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6756)). Wheatley, Wehrli and Sornette then fitted a flexible baseline chosen by BIC and "strongly reject" criticality [V] ([Wheatley et al.](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14697688.2018.1550266)). The count-variance estimator behind the repo's 0.96 belongs to the Hardiman and Bouchaud family [R] ([arXiv 1403.5227](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.5227)). The notes' reading is that 0.71 is a lower bound from kernel truncation and 0.96 an upper bound contaminated by baseline non-stationarity [I]. A crypto datapoint exists: Fabre and Muni Toke report a system branching ratio near 0.80 with a power-law kernel on a centralised exchange [V, search extracts] ([Fabre and Muni Toke](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.09361)); their "Neural Hawkes" uses a network to solve for a kernel inside a linear Hawkes and is not a black-box sequence model.

On neural temporal point processes, the independent evidence is cautious. The EasyTPP benchmark states "no consistent winner" among neural models [V] ([EasyTPP slides](https://iclr.cc/media/iclr-2024/Slides/18712_vJKTJVC.pdf)). A 15-dataset study finds the output head, not the history encoder, drives timing accuracy [V] ([Bosser and Ben Taieb](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.17066v2)). A re-evaluation accepted at NeurIPS 2026 concludes that the reported neural advantage comes from comparing against Hawkes processes "in their most restricted form," and that with flexible baselines and kernels "the previously reported gap largely disappears" [V, abstract level; a very recent preprint not read in full] ([Mehrtens et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06348)). The standard review warns that likelihood "can be 'fooled'" when timestamps are measured in discrete units, because a flexible density can place narrow spikes on the lattice [V] ([Shchur et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.03528v5)). Binance timestamps are discrete and tied, so this warning applies directly.

The model choice follows from mechanics. The log-normal-mixture head of Shchur, Bilos and Gunnemann models the next waiting time directly, with closed-form likelihood, sampling and moments [V] ([Shchur et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.12127)). Neural Hawkes, Transformer Hawkes, SAHP and AttNHP need Monte Carlo integration for every likelihood evaluation [R] ([Mei and Eisner](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.09328); [Zuo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09291)), and FullyNN defines a density that does not integrate to one [V] ([Shchur et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.12127)). So Q12 should proceed in two steps [I]. Step one, with no neural network: extend the kernel to 8 to 15 exponentials on a fixed log-spaced rate grid, add a piecewise-constant baseline selected by BIC, and report how the branching ratio moves between 0.85 and 0.96 as the baseline gains flexibility. That decomposition is the main result. Step two: a GRU with 32 to 64 units and a mixture head of 8 to 16 components, fed the multi-timescale event-rate averages that are themselves Hawkes states, used as a ceiling on held-out likelihood.

The comparison protocol must put both model families on identical footing [I]: the same held-out events, the same warm-up history, scores reported as nats per event relative to a Poisson reference so that business-time rescaling constants cancel, and an interval-censored likelihood (the probability of the observed millisecond bin, available in closed form for both families) so ties cannot be exploited. Residual checks use the time-rescaling theorem, under which a correct model's compensator increments are independent unit exponentials [V] ([Brown et al. 2002](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11555987_The_Time-Rescaling_Theorem_and_Its_Application_to_Neural_Spike_Train_Data_Analysis)); formal tests reject nearly every Hawkes fit on long financial samples [V] ([Lallouache and Challet](https://hal.science/hal-01134105)), so report distances and residual autocorrelation, not p-values. Before any real-data claim, simulate the repo's fitted two-exponential Hawkes, round to milliseconds, and confirm that the neural model recovers the known likelihood; if it cannot, its real-data output is not interpretable.

A neural point process has no branching ratio. The quantity is defined for the linear Hawkes cluster structure, and the notes found no source for estimating it from a black-box model [V for the absence in the review literature] ([Shchur et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.03528)). The neural model therefore cannot adjudicate among 0.71, 0.85 and 0.96; that dispute concerns long memory versus baseline non-stationarity, which a short-memory recurrent network does not see [I]. It answers one question only: how much held-out likelihood the linear Hawkes family leaves unexplained.

## Everything fits on four cores if rows are subsampled in blocks

No source in the notes gives CPU timings at this scale, so every figure in this section is an unmeasured engineering estimate [I], and the notes assumed 4 to 16 cores where the project has 4. The Yule-Walker filter and logistic regressions are trivial. Histogram-based boosted trees suit tabular data on CPU, but the notes' figure of 100 million rows assumed more cores and memory than this machine has; at float32, 30 features over 10 million rows is about 1.2 GB, which fits in 15 GB with room for the library's working copies, so Q9 to Q11 should train on 5 to 10 million rows per month drawn as contiguous blocks of days from a fixed symbol panel, and evaluate on full held-out days. The domain classifier for Q10 needs only 100 to 500 thousand rows per month. The Hawkes extension costs order N times J per likelihood evaluation, seconds at a million events. For the neural model the notes estimate on the order of 100 thousand events per second for a small GRU, which would allow tens of epochs on 1 to 5 million events in under an hour; with 4 cores, assume the low end and run a ten-minute benchmark before committing. Events must never be subsampled individually for point-process work, because thinning changes the process; subsample contiguous blocks. Transformers and Monte Carlo likelihood models are out of budget and unsupported by the evidence. A deep sequence model for Q9 to Q11 is not recommended: the literature gives little reason to expect a gain over boosted trees on these inputs.

## What can and cannot honestly be claimed

Statements the project can make if the protocol above is followed: that it measured the out-of-sample skill of standard ML models for next-trade sign against a theory-derived linear baseline, with dependence-aware intervals; that it measured how that skill and its increment changed between 2023 and 2026, and split the change into staleness and lost predictability; that in 2023 it tested whether the predictable part of order flow moves the mid-price; and that it measured how much held-out likelihood a neural point process adds over a flexible Hawkes model. Each is a measurement, and a null result (ML adds nothing over the filter; the neural model adds under 0.01 nats per event) is as publishable as a positive one and more consistent with the benchmark literature.

Statements the project cannot make: that any model is profitable or tradeable, since no execution simulation with queue position exists and the fee hurdle is about 10 basis points per taker round trip at the regular tier; that 60 to 75% sign accuracy indicates market inefficiency, since theory predicts it; that mid-price predictability persists after 2023, since there is no quote data to test it; that results on a single test month (July 2023) generalise across regimes; that a neural model "has" a branching ratio; or that the work replicates or beats DeepLOB-class models, which need ten book levels the project does not have. Literature numbers tagged [R] or [2nd] in this report should not be repeated publicly until checked against the primary papers, and the fee figures should be confirmed on Binance's own schedule.

## Conclusion

The shape of the opportunity is the reverse of what the deep-learning literature advertises. The repo's existing estimators (sign autocorrelation, propagator, Hawkes) are not things for ML to replace; they are the baselines that make an ML result interpretable, and in three of the four proposed questions the notes found no published work that makes the comparison on this market. The quote-data gap, which looks like a handicap, also forces a clean design: trade-only features give a four-year drift study with labels in every period, and the 2023 quote window gives one tightly scoped test of the surprise mechanism with a pre-committed test month.

The sequencing risk is that most of the value sits in the harness and the baselines, which are the least glamorous parts. If time runs short, Q9 and Q10 alone form a complete study that ties directly to the Q8 sign-flip result, and Q12a alone resolves more of the branching-ratio question than any neural model can. Before publication, the remaining work is verification: re-reading the primary sources behind every [R] and [2nd] tag once full-text access is available, measuring the tick regime and timestamp behaviour on the repo's own data, and benchmarking the two unmeasured compute assumptions.
